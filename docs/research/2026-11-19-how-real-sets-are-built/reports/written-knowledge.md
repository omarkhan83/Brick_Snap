## 2. What LEGO's designers say about their own rules

### 2.1 Connections: the "legal / illegal" rule set
- LEGO states it outright: "The LEGO Group has internal design rules for connecting LEGO pieces together for stability and to avoid unnecessary stress." [read, S2]
- The word "illegal" goes back to Berard's 2006 talk; BrickNerd reports he later said it "was never meant to be official". [read, S3, reporting Berard second-hand]
- A connection is ruled out if it joins parts **too tightly, too loosely, or damages them**. [read, S3 and Blocks magazine]. Stafford: an illegal build is one where "the LEGO elements are stressed", "not acceptable in a toy". [read, S15]
- Berard's general principles (slide 26, text) [read, S1]:
  - "Heat Test"
  - "Different Plastics = Different Rules"
  - "Can it be built and played with by a 7 year old?"
  - "Can one brick replace the need for 5? – Reduces Complexity – Adds Strength and Stability – Reduces Tolerance Accumulation"
  - "Technic Half-Beams and System Plates are not friends"
  - "Support Everything!"
- The detailed cases are in section 4 below.

### 2.2 How sturdiness is checked
- **Model Committee build-through.** After the 2002 Audi TT promotional set ("forces elements into unnatural connections causing them to be permanently damaged"; it shipped "with only a single picture and no building instructions"), "all models now produced by the LEGO Group must go through the Design Department … approved by a Model Committee. Sometimes lasting up to 2 or 3 days, this review allows representatives from building instructions, senior designers, engineers and the designer sit down together and build the model." [read, S1 slide 31]
- **Heat test.** "A final heat test then highlights further weaknesses that must be changed before the set can be launched." [read, S1]. Also from a LEGO employee: "We have tests where we heat [the set] up and make sure it can sit on a window sill" and "not fall apart if it gets too warm". [read, S16]
- **Rules by age.** "We have building rules, we have testing rules, we have age marks … if you're building a set for 4-to-6-year-olds, there's a lot of stuff you can't do." (Carl Merriam, LEGO) [read, S16]
- **Weight limits.** On the large T. rex: the bigger prototype "is actually too heavy for LEGO hinges"; "even the Voltron-style hinge, it was too heavy"; "There's only so much weight our System can withstand." [read, S12]
- A drop test is **not** mentioned in any LEGO source I found. MIT's robot course uses one (a 6-foot drop of a cube at least 10 studs on a side) [read, S18], but that is not LEGO practice.
- Clutch is specified in newtons but the target number is secret, according to Brick Architect's article on LEGO's clutch test tools. [second-hand, https://brickarchitect.com/2021/lego-clutch-test-implements-bricks/ via search summary]

### 2.3 What makes a good build for the person following the instructions
- The instructions team "decides on the individual steps, how many pieces to add in each step, and the angle shown in the booklet"; they build the model "many, many times in different ways" and "start building from the ground up". [read, S14]
- Sets get "input on buildability by our target age group from our building instructions experts"; a part combination that "can't be clearly explained in building instructions" stays out of sets. [read, S15]
- The instructions team is involved early in design and again after release. For the largest sets one person may spend over a year on the instructions. [read, Brick Architect fan day notes]
- **Bright hidden parts:** a former LEGO designer writes the reason is "to make the building experience better" by contrast with the surrounding bricks, and that "lowering the time to find bricks" is scrutinised during design. He rejects "using up stock" and "cheaper colours" as reasons. [read, S13]. A LEGO Star Wars designer says much the same: a bright brick among grey helps the eye find the part (César Soares). [second-hand, search summary of Brick Fanatics]
- **Sub-assemblies, steps of a few parts, mirrored steps:** I found **no** LEGO statement giving a number of parts per step or a rule for sub-assemblies or mirrored steps. The booklets themselves are the evidence (other researchers' parts). The 1998 study does say LEGO's model builders build "one layer at a time and … once a layer was completed it would be left fixed". [read, S5 p. 87]. That describes large display models, not boxed sets.
- Agrawala et al. (cognitive design principles for assembly instructions) found instructions designed this way cut assembly time by about 35% and errors by 50%. That figure is from their follow-up paper. [second-hand]

### 2.4 How a new model is started
- Designers "build sketch models that are buildable in LEGO bricks", starting from ideas that are "often glued together and featuring found or created elements". [read, S15]
- A sketch model is made "just to sell the idea internally"; "we can do whatever" (sprayed, glued, 3D-printed parts). It is then handed to someone who turns it into a real set, "trying to eliminate as many new elements being needed as possible". [read, S17]
- Mike Psiaki: "We do nearly everything starting with building bricks." [read, S16]. Stuart Harris (LEGO) describes a mood board, then sketches, then "basic CAD models to mass the model out", then detailing in bricks. [second-hand, GeekDad via search summary]. So sources differ on whether paper sketches come first.
- **The scale-setting part.** For vehicles this is stated plainly: "the only thing that matters is the diameter of the wheels (together with tires)". The largest component (a battery box, for instance) sets the minimum size in another direction. [read, S19, a fan expert]. For the T. rex, the designer kept the original prototype's scale because "he'd chosen it for a good reason". [read, S12]. Speed Champions moved to 8 studs wide so the cars could be "as low and as wide as possible". [second-hand, search summary of Brickset and New Elementary interviews with Chris Stamp]
- **From the core.** No LEGO designer quote I found says "start with the core". The closest are: the poppy in 10280 uses "a simple core that will just hold it all together" [read, S11], and Psiaki is reported as thinking about how the first version "could be built from the bottom up". [second-hand]

## 3. Consolidated rules for an AI builder (most certain first)

Each rule is written as an instruction, with its source. "Certain" means LEGO itself said it. Further down the list the rules come from builders or research.

**From LEGO itself**
1. Every connection must be one the parts were designed for. Never force a part into a place where it is squeezed, bent or held open (S1, S2, S15).
2. Click hinges and other ratchet joints must sit at a click position. Use angles in multiples of 22.5° (S1 slide 16: "Click hinges must be in 'click.' Approved angles are in multiples of 22.5 degrees").
3. A Technic pin must go fully home, into a hole made for it, so that it clicks. A pin that is half in, or that sits in a round hole without the click groove, is not allowed (S1 slides 8, 10, 29).
4. A stud in a Technic hole is allowed only as a single stud. Never join several studs into several holes, and never put further parts across the top or bottom of that join (S1 slides 4, 12: "a single stud into a single Technic hole and a child can still take them apart. Any more than that and the resistance becomes too great").
5. Never wedge a plate or another part between studs, or into a gap narrower than the part (S1 slides 24, 28: "the receiving brick has smaller dimensions than the one being connected to it"). The exception: a tile between studs is tolerated because it is thinner than a plate (S3).
6. Never slide a transparent part along another transparent part, such as a bar through a transparent cone. Both are polycarbonate, and "there is little hope of a child pulling them apart" (S1 slide 20).
7. Keep every assembly "in system". Two plates joined some other way must still sit at a whole number of plates in height, so avoid joints like a towball on a ball-socket plate that leave them at different heights (S1 slide 22).
8. Support everything. No part may depend on friction alone without a seat under it (S1 slide 26).
9. Prefer one larger part to several small ones where it does the same job. It is simpler, stronger, and adds up fewer tolerances (S1 slide 26).
10. The finished model must survive warmth (a heat test) and being built and played with by a child of the stated age (S1, S16).
11. Hinges hold only limited weight. A long, heavy part on a hinge or click joint (a neck, a tail, a big wing) will droop, so make the part lighter, shorten it, or support it another way (S12).
12. Hide parts in bright contrasting colours where they help the builder find the right part, and keep the outside in the model's colours (S13).
13. Avoid mixing Technic half-beams with ordinary plates in one stack (S1 slide 26, no reason given on the slide).

**From LEGO's model builders as reported by the 1998 study (S5), confirmed by research (S6)**

14. Use the largest bricks that fit. Bigger bricks, and bricks with more studs connected to other bricks, make a stronger model (S5 p. 81: "More stable models are those which are made with bigger bricks and with bricks that have more studs connected to other bricks").
15. Turn the bricks in alternate layers at right angles where they overlap (S5 factor 3; S7 chapter 7: "Alternating the orientation of the bricks from one layer to the next … will yield the strongest result").
16. Never let a seam between two bricks in one layer fall directly under a seam in the next layer. Cover every vertical seam with a brick above and below, ideally centred on the seam (S5 factors 4–6, penalties P2 and P3).
17. Where a joint makes a T, put the middle of the crossing brick against the joint (S5 factor 5, P3).
18. Fill thick areas with a herringbone of bricks, shifted one stud in both directions in the next layer (S5 p. 84). The study's own scoring prefers alternating brick-wall layers to herringbone (p. 89).
19. Make large models hollow, with a roughly box-shaped space inside so a support or a mechanism can go there, and keep a shell 2 to 6 bricks thick (S5 pp. 81–82). Research found a shell 2 studs thick "usually … sufficient for stability even for complex models" (S6).
20. Build each layer from the outside edge first, then the inner edge, then fill between (S5 p. 82).
21. Small changes to the shape are acceptable "if the change produces a model which still 'looks good' and the change is beneficial in some other way, for example if it increases the stability" (S5 p. 82).
22. No part of the model may hang by a single brick. Find any brick whose removal would split the model into two pieces of more than one brick (a "weak articulation point") and rearrange around it (S6).
23. Visible outer bricks of different colours must stay separate. Inner bricks can merge regardless of colour (S6).

**From experienced builders**

24. Sideways sections must fill a whole number of plates. Two studs of width equal five plates of height, so build sideways sections in steps of 2 studs, or use a bracket (half a plate thick) or a headlight brick's half-plate inset for odd widths (S4, S7, S23).
25. To brace a Technic beam vertically, put two plates between two Technic bricks. That makes 2 studs of height, so the holes line up with a vertical beam (S18 "beam-flat-flat-beam or 3-2-3 rule").
26. Diagonals must follow a Pythagorean triple, such as 3-4-5, 6-8-10 or 5-12-13, counted between stud or hole centres. Otherwise the parts are forced (S4, S18, S22: 3:4:5 used in official set 10297).
27. Long spans need a continuous long member inside (a long brick, a long plate or a Technic beam), with plate seams staggered over supports and large plates anchored to the walls. Plates alone make weak roofs (forum consensus, [second-hand]; S9 chapter "load-bearing structures… rails, chassis, and body frames… trusses").
28. A round wall on a grid must have one pair of opposite sides on the grid. Pick side counts whose diameter comes out a whole number of studs (S7).
29. A hinged round wall moves until it is held. Build an inner wall of ordinary bricks right behind it and tie the two together sideways (S7).
30. For a smooth sphere, build a cube with studs in all six directions and put six identical curved panels on it (Lowell sphere). For a stacked sphere, make the height 5/6 of the width so that it comes out round (S4, S7).
31. Set the scale from the part you cannot change: wheels for vehicles, the minifigure for buildings, the largest bought part otherwise (S19, S10, S7 chapter 2).
32. For very large display models, put a steel frame inside a hollow brick shell 2 to 10 studs thick, and brace the shell against the steel with bricks (S20). This is outside BrickSnap's scope but explains why giant sculptures are possible.

### 4.3 Cases that look fine but are known to be used, or were once legal
- **Was legal, now illegal:** grey "cannons" attached by 1×2 plates wedged between two studs in an old set (S1 slides 27–28).
- **Made it to market:** a Technic set with pins "not in 'click'", which "weakens the build resulting in potential failure during play" (S1 slide 29).
- **Bent rules in official sets:** a stud in a Technic hole used for a sideways roof (10222), for decorations (71040) and for structure via a stud on an axle (21311), as BrickNerd reports (S3). So rule 4 is a guideline LEGO bends. A checker should flag it as a warning, not reject it.
- A 2×2 round brick with flutes sits on studs at 45° (Ward calls it a "surprise" fit). An old 2×2 round tile with a cross underside fits on a 1×1, which Ward calls "weird & illegal" (S4 pp. 64–65).

## 6. Method by kind of subject (as builders describe it)

These are composites. Each step names its source; a step with no source is my link between sources and is marked [inferred]. The step-by-step booklet evidence is in the other researchers' reports.

### 6.1 Vehicles (studded, minifigure to Speed Champions scale)
1. Choose the wheels first; the wheel diameter fixes the scale. Use wheels 10–20% larger than scale where the bodywork hides part of the tyre (S19).
2. Work out width from the axles and wheels (S19). At minifigure scale, check that the figure fits: a seated figure with a helmet is 11 plates tall, so add floor, roof and ground clearance (14 plates in BrickNerd's six-wide guide) [second-hand, https://bricknerd.com/home/six-wide-how-to-fit-a-minifig-in-a-custom-car-10-24-24].
3. Build the chassis as one strong layer everything else attaches to. LEGO itself designs special chassis parts for this (the Speed Champions chassis went through 22 versions and is paired with a wheel-arch part) [second-hand, Stamp interviews].
4. Round all dimensions to half studs at most (S19).
5. Build the sides and nose sideways on bricks with studs on their sides, keeping sideways sections to steps of 2 studs = 5 plates (S4, S7 TOC "SNOT").
6. Use half-plate offsets (headlight bricks, brackets) and stepped cheese slopes for gentle slopes on bonnets and noses (S4 pp. 31–37).
7. Doors, bonnet and similar parts: clip-and-bar or hinges, at click angles if click hinges (S1).
8. Large Technic vehicles: the drivetrain and gearbox are designed together with the styling. For the Chiron, chassis and body are joined at the end ("marriage") [second-hand, interviews]. Build frames from Technic beams, braced by Pythagorean triangles, and reinforce casings around gears (S9 "building strong", S18).

### 6.2 Buildings
1. Choose the scale from the minifigure or from a footprint (S10, S7 ch. 2 "Modeling a house / skyscraper").
2. Plan the floor first (fan guidance, [second-hand]). In modular sets some features are fixed: connector positions, lamp post, sidewalk tiling (S22).
3. Build walls in a staggered bond and lock corners with L- and T-junctions (S7 TOC ch. 3; S5 ideal walls).
4. Offset the vertical joints and add internal supports in tall buildings (S7 TOC ch. 3 "offsetting vertical joints", "internal support structures").
5. Use as few and as large plates as possible for upper floors, since small ones bow [second-hand], and anchor them to the walls [second-hand].
6. Recessed windows and panels: jumper plates for half-stud insets, headlight bricks or brackets for half-plate insets (S4 pp. 21, 39; S7 TOC ch. 4).
7. Façades and sideways details: brackets or SNOT bricks at 2-stud steps (S4 pp. 24–25).
8. Angled walls: hinge plates, turntables, or Pythagorean triangles (3:4:5 used in 10297) (S7 TOC ch. 6; S22).
9. Roofs: slopes or stepped bricks (S7 TOC ch. 3 "creating sloped roofs").
10. Floors that lift off are separate sections that stack (S22).

### 6.3 Creatures and figures
1. Fix scale and proportion from the subject; choose where it must move (S8 ch. 8–9 "Scale, Proportion, Articulation, Expression").
2. Build a body core with studs in several directions (SNOT bricks), and add plates and curved slopes as skin. This is reported for the 75936 head and body [second-hand, Brothers Brick review via search summary]. S7 ch. 9 calls it Lowell-style panels around a SNOT core applied "beyond spheres".
3. Attach heavy limbs, neck and tail with large click hinges or ball joints, and keep the heavy parts short or light: hinges have a weight limit (S12).
4. Make tails and necks curve with chains of hinged segments at fixed spacing (S12: the tail spacing "allows it to have this wonderful curve").
5. Check balance early: the first T. rex prototype "was already unable to stand!" (S12).
6. Small animals: use prefabricated animal parts or a few shaped bricks (S8 ch. 8 "Creating Small Animals", "Prefabricated Creatures").
7. Character joints: builders use clips, bars, ball joints and click hinges. Clips and a T-bar on a 1×1 brick are "great when you need a bit more strength" (BrickNerd *Joint Effort*, https://bricknerd.com/home/joint-effort-lego-joint-techniques-for-articulated-builds-1-17-25 [read]).
8. Hollow the body where weight matters (S6 shell 2) [inferred for creatures].

### 6.4 Plants (botanical)
1. Treat each flower as its own small sub-assembly; "the flowers kind of just exist in the 3D space, so it's not necessarily obvious where to start" (S11, second-hand phrasing in search summary; the S11 page I read said "the flowers started… more organically").
2. Make long stems from Technic axles ("The 32 module, Technic axle just became the go-to part"); thicker stems from Technic connectors, which also lets the length be adjusted (S11).
3. Use a small solid core to hold the petals ("a simple core that will just hold it all together", poppy) (S11).
4. Attach petals and leaves on clips and bars, so they can be turned and posed (S11: aster from "small leaf pieces on clips"; Brick Fanatics on bars and clips for branches [second-hand]).
5. Check the friction of axles through round parts. The aster centre was changed because the axle was "too hard, with too much friction, to push … through" (S11).
6. Keep top-heavy stems short enough not to "fall over in your hands"; place leaves so they catch on the rim of a vase (S11).
7. Reuse parts for other jobs: Technic gears as millet stalks [second-hand, Milan Madge via search summary].

### 6.5 Spheres, rounded bodies, domes
1. Choose the method by size and finish:
   - stacked bricks, blocky (S7);
   - stacked plates, smoother from the side but blocky from above (S7);
   - Lowell sphere, smooth in all directions (S4, S7);
   - curved slopes on a SNOT core, for cylinders (S7).
2. Stacked: take a circle chart for each layer; make the height 5/6 of the width (bricks) or the plate-equivalent; walls at least 2 studs thick; turn the bricks in alternate layers (S7).
3. Lowell sphere: build a cube with studs in all six directions; build six identical panels from plates, each slightly longer than wide so they interlock; "just a handful of connection points for each of the panels is usually sufficient" (S7).
4. Replace the 1×1 plates from a generator with larger interlocking plates, so that seams do not line up between layers (S7).
5. For a dome, drop the bottom panel, shorten the core, cut the side panels flat (S7).
6. For an egg or other oval, stretch some panels; the Lowell principle also works for bodies such as a teddy bear (S4 pp. 59–61).
7. Round walls: alternate 1×3 bricks with 1×1 round bricks as hinges, or use hinge plates in two layers. Pick a side count that comes out a whole number of studs across, and back the wall with an inner wall (S7).

### 6.6 Sculptures (studs-up, from a 3D shape)
1. Voxelise the shape at a chosen size (S6, S20).
2. Hollow it: keep a shell of about 2 studs (S6), or 2–6 bricks (S5). Professionals use 2 studs in software and 6–10 studs in practice around steel (S20).
3. Leave a box-shaped interior space for supports (S5).
4. Merge the 1×1 cells into the largest bricks that fit, preferring merges that make more connections; change brick direction between layers; cover the seams (S5, S6).
5. Keep visible colours separate; inside, ignore colour (S6).
6. Check the brick graph: one connected piece, no brick whose removal splits two groups (weak articulation point). Split and re-merge around problems; thin parts such as ears may need the shape changed (S6).
7. Remove interior bricks that do not weaken it (about 20% fewer bricks in S6).
8. Build one layer at a time, from the outer edge in (S5, S20).
9. Very large: a steel frame inside, bricks braced to the steel, made in sections, glued (S20; LEGOLAND glue [second-hand]).

### 6.7 Technic frames (cores of pins and beams)
1. Decide studfull, studless or both (S9 "studless or studfull? … combining the styles").
2. Use the half stud as the smallest unit; watch even and odd lengths (S9 "even vs. odd", "the half stud as the minimum building unit").
3. Brace vertical Technic-brick stacks with two plates between (2-stud pitch) and cross-connect with vertical beams (S18).
4. Triangulate with Pythagorean triples; otherwise angles add "unnecessary shear forces", especially around gearboxes (S18).
5. Find weak links, reinforce where the load is, use rails, chassis frames and trusses (S9 "building strong" section list).
6. Every pin fully in click (S1).

## 7. Where sources disagree

- **How far up the Technic hole sits.** Berard (LEGO) gives System side-stud centre 3.92 mm and Technic hole centre 3.80 mm from the top, a 0.12 mm difference [S1]. Ward says "about 0.2 mm" [S4]. BrickNerd says 0.1 mm [S3, S23]. Berard's figure is the primary one.
- **Counting Pythagorean triangles.** Ward: add one stud to each side, so 3-4-5 becomes 4-5-6 studs [S4]. MIT counts "nubs" and lists 3-4-5 directly [S18]. Both are right if MIT means distance between hole centres, but a program must count centre-to-centre.
- **Stud in a Technic hole.** Berard: legal only alone, not recommended [S1]. Ward: "not 'legal' for set designers" [S4]. BrickNerd: tolerated for single decorative studs, and used structurally in 21311 [S3].
- **Clip on one plate.** BrickNerd: old clip forced open was illegal; newer clip "considered legal nowadays" [S3]. Berard shows a clip on a plate edge as legal [S1, picture].
- **Do sketches come first?** Harris: mood board, sketches, CAD massing [second-hand]. Psiaki: "nearly everything starting with building bricks" [S16]. Stafford: sketch models in bricks, glued [S15, S17].
- **Shell thickness for hollow models.** 2–6 unit bricks (LEGO builders, S5); 2 studs enough (S6); 2 studs in software but 6–10 studs in practice around steel (S20). The answer depends on size and on whether there is a steel frame.
- **Herringbone fill.** LEGO's builders told the 1998 study herringbone is good for thick areas [S5]. The study's own scoring prefers alternating brick-wall layers [S5 p. 89].
- **Stud diameter.** 4.8 mm in most sources, 5 mm in one older FAQ, 4.87–4.90 mm measured [all second-hand].
- **Transparent parts.** Berard: friction between polycarbonate parts makes sliding joints impossible to undo [S1]. Some fan pages claim a "chemical bond" hazard [second-hand]. That claim is not in the LEGO source.

## 8. What nobody seems to have written down

- **A number of parts per step, or a rule for sub-assemblies and mirrored steps.** LEGO says the instructions team decides these; no figure or rule is published. The booklets themselves are the evidence.
- **"Start from the core" as a stated method.** No designer quote I found says it. Designers talk about sketch models, scale, and making the model buildable "from the bottom up". The core-first habit has to be shown from booklets (the other parts of this study).
- **When a Technic frame is used instead of bricks inside a studded set.** Nothing published. Only Berard's "not friends" (half-beams and plates) and the 2-stud vertical pitch rule bear on it.
- **Numeric sturdiness criteria.** LEGO's clutch target is secret. There is no published rule such as "a span over N studs needs a lock plate", no published hinge-torque limits, and no drop-test standard. The only LEGO tests named are the build-through and the heat test.
- **A complete list of LEGO's legal and illegal connections.** Berard's 2006 slides are the only primary list, and most cases are shown as pictures. LEGO's current internal rules are not public, and the rules changed over time ("legal back then, illegal now").
- **Ways to stop sagging in medium-size official sets** (as opposed to giant display models with steel). Only forum advice.
- **Creature cores in official sets.** Interviews mention balance and hinges, not the internal structure.
- **Books' method in their own words.** I used only tables of contents and publisher descriptions, plus one free sample chapter. The chapter titles show the method (staggered bonds, internal supports, half-stud and half-plate offsets, Pythagorean walls, Lowell spheres, studless frames, trusses), but not the authors' reasons.

## 9. For BrickSnap: what bears directly on design method

- The checker's rules can start from section 4.2: interference (wedging), pins fully seated, at most one stud–hole join between rigid groups, click hinges on 22.5°, nothing out of the plate grid, no sliding transparent-on-transparent joints.
- The 1998 study's six factors are LEGO's own builders' rules for solid volumes, and Testuz's weak articulation point is the matching graph test. Together they describe what the solver should score: big bricks, alternating direction, covered seams, centred on T-joints, a single connected graph with no single-brick bridges, and a 2-stud shell with a box-shaped hollow.
- Section 5's table (2 studs = 5 plates, 2-stud vertical Technic pitch, Pythagorean counts centre-to-centre, 22.5° clicks) is what a sideways- or angle-placing tool must respect.
